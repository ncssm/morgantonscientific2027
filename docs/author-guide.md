# Morganton Scientific — Author's Guide

**Write in whatever you like.** Google Docs and Word are both fine. Our technical editors convert
the final manuscript into the publishing format, so you don't need to learn any markup.

What follows is everything you *can* supply that makes publication smoother — and the handful of
habits that cause the most rework when they're missing. Most of it takes minutes and can only be
done by you.

---

## 1. Information only you can give us

Please include all of this, either at the top of your document or on a separate cover page.

### Your ORCID

An ORCID is a permanent digital identifier that ties your work to you, and stays with you across
schools, jobs and name changes. It is free and takes about two minutes:
**[orcid.org/register](https://orcid.org/register)**.

Register **before** you submit. It becomes part of the permanent DOI record for your article, and
adding it afterwards is far more work than adding it now.

Give it in full: `0009-0006-7158-659X`. If you have co-authors, every one of them needs their own.

> **Co-authors from outside NCSSM?** Tell us early. If an ORCID genuinely can't be obtained, the
> usual outcome is that the contributor is thanked in the Acknowledgements rather than listed as an
> author — better to make that decision deliberately than at the last minute.

### Your contribution — CRediT roles

For each author, list which of these applied. Most student papers use two or three; a sole author
often uses more.

| | |
|---|---|
| Conceptualization | the ideas, the research question |
| Data curation | managing and organizing the data |
| Formal analysis | statistics, modelling, the maths |
| Funding acquisition | securing grants or materials |
| Investigation | running the experiments |
| Methodology | designing how it would be done |
| Project administration | coordinating and scheduling |
| Resources | supplying materials, samples, equipment |
| Software | code you wrote |
| Supervision | overseeing others' work |
| Validation | verifying that the results hold up |
| Visualization | making the figures |
| Writing – original draft | writing the first version |
| Writing – review & editing | revising, responding to feedback |

Write it plainly, e.g.:

> **Author contributions.** J. Smith: Conceptualization, Investigation, Formal analysis,
> Writing – original draft. A. Patel: Methodology, Validation, Writing – review & editing.

This is a **required field** for publication. It's also the single item most often missing —
in Volume 3, not one of the fifteen submissions arrived with it, and it became the last thing
holding up the volume.

### Corresponding author

Name one author as the point of contact for readers after publication, and give the email address
you'll still be able to read after you graduate. If your paper has one author, that's you.

### Abbreviations

List every abbreviation you use, with what it stands for:

```
TBI   Traumatic Brain Injury
DPPH  2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl
OKR   Optokinetic Response
```

Two rules:

- **List only abbreviations you actually use in the text.** If a term appears once, spell it out and
  drop the abbreviation.
- **Define it in the prose, not in a heading.** The first time it appears in a sentence, write it out
  in full with the abbreviation in parentheses — "the High Impact Trauma (HIT) device" — then use
  the short form after that. A definition that only exists in a section heading is easy for a reader
  to skip past.

### Keywords

**Five, maximum.** Choose the terms someone would search for to find work like yours. Prefer the
specific over the general: "Negative geotaxis" is more useful than "Biology". Avoid words already in
your title — they're indexed anyway.

## 2. Figures, tables and charts

These three words are not interchangeable, and getting them right matters for how your work is
numbered, captioned and referenced.

| | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Table** | Rows and columns of values | Participant demographics, p-values, a results matrix |
| **Figure** | Anything visual | Photographs, micrographs, diagrams, gels, apparatus |
| **Chart / graph** | A *kind of* figure — data drawn as a picture | Bar chart, scatter plot, line graph |

A chart is a figure. **A table is not.** Tables and figures are numbered in separate sequences —
Table 1, Table 2 alongside Figure 1, Figure 2 — so labelling a table as a figure throws both
sequences out.

### Send tables as text, never as pictures

If it has rows and columns, give it to us as an actual table — in the document, or as a spreadsheet.
**Do not paste a screenshot of a table.**

A screenshotted table can't be searched, can't be read by a screen reader, can't be copied by
someone building on your work, and goes blurry in print. In Volume 3 three "figures" turned out to
be screenshots of statistical tables, and re-typing them by hand risked introducing errors into
published statistics.

### Refer to every figure in your text

Every figure and table must be mentioned at least once in the prose — that mention is what tells the
reader *why* it's there.

> Climbing performance declined at the highest dose (Figure 4).

This is the most common gap we see. In Volume 3, **36 of 84 figures and tables were never mentioned
anywhere in the text**, and three papers referenced none of their own figures at all. A figure no
one refers to reads as decoration.

### Number them in the order they first appear

Figure 1 should be the first figure mentioned. If you reorder your paper, renumber. Two Volume 3
papers had every reference off by one — the text said "Figure 9" where it meant the eighth figure —
which took real detective work to untangle.

### Every figure and table needs a caption

A caption should let someone who reads nothing else understand what they're looking at: what's
shown, what the conditions were, what the error bars mean, and how many replicates.

> **Figure 3.** Negative geotaxis performance after 10 days on each diet. Bars show mean ± SEM,
> n = 8 vials per group. \*p < 0.05, \*\*p < 0.01.

Don't type "Figure 3." into the caption yourself — numbering is applied automatically, and a typed
number will end up duplicated.

### Send figures at full resolution

Export the original at the largest size available — **300 dpi**, or at least 2000 pixels on the long
edge. Don't paste a screenshot of a chart, and don't shrink an image to fit the page in Word before
sending it. Once resolution is gone it can't be restored, and it's the difference between a crisp
figure and a soft one in print.

### Figures you didn't make

If a figure is from a published paper, a website, or anywhere else, **tell us**. The journal is
published open access under CC-BY, which lets readers redistribute what we publish — something we
can only grant for material we own.

Citing the source is not the same as having permission. If a figure is central to your argument,
the cleanest route is usually to redraw it with your own data, or make the point in prose.

## 3. Citations and the bibliography

### Use a reference manager

Zotero (free), Mendeley or EndNote. Export a `.bib` file and send it with your manuscript. Manual
bibliographies are where most citation problems begin.

### Two rules that catch almost everything

1. **Everything you cite must be in your bibliography.** A citation with no entry can't be resolved,
   and tracking down what a student meant months later is often impossible.
2. **Everything in your bibliography must be cited.** An uncited entry never appears in the
   reference list — it silently vanishes. If it's there because you read it and it shaped your
   thinking, cite it. If not, delete it.

Volume 3 had **60 uncited entries across 10 papers**, and one citation whose source we were never
able to identify with confidence.

### Include DOIs

Every reference that has one. It's the permanent link, and we check that they resolve. Books,
theses, older papers and websites often have none — that's fine, just flag them so we know it's
expected rather than an oversight.

### Cite where the claim is made

Put the citation next to the sentence it supports, not at the end of a paragraph covering four
sources.

## 4. Your abstract

**Aim for 180–250 words; 400 is the hard limit.** It should stand alone — someone reading only the
abstract should come away knowing what you did, what you found, and why it matters.

Lead with your own work rather than a paragraph of background. Compare:

> Caffeine is one of the most widely consumed substances worldwide…

with:

> Wild-type *Drosophila melanogaster* were reared on diets containing 0.01–0.05% caffeine and
> assessed for survival, climbing performance and short-term memory…

The second tells a reader browsing a list of articles whether yours is relevant.

Don't cite in the abstract, and don't use abbreviations you haven't spelled out there.

## 5. Title, names and small things that are hard to fix later

- **Proofread your title.** It's the most visible text you'll write, it goes into the permanent DOI
  record, and it's the most expensive thing to correct after publication. One Volume 3 title
  reached final review reading "Pronation" where it meant "Protonation".
- **No full stop at the end of a title.**
- **Use one form of your name**, consistently, everywhere — in the document, in your email, on your
  ORCID. "A. L. Qu", "Alvin Qu" and "Alvin L. Qu" are three different people to an indexing system.
- **Species names** are italicised, with a lowercase species epithet: *Drosophila melanogaster*,
  *Caenorhabditis elegans*, *Danio rerio*. Capital genus, lowercase species — always.
- **American spelling** throughout: modeled, behavior, analyze, aging, characterized.
- **Headings** shouldn't end with a colon, and shouldn't be numbered by hand.

## Before you submit — a two-minute check

- [ ] Every author has an ORCID, and I've included them
- [ ] CRediT roles listed for every author
- [ ] Corresponding author named, with an email that will still work after graduation
- [ ] Five keywords or fewer
- [ ] Abbreviation list included; every one is used in the text and defined in prose on first use
- [ ] Every figure and table is mentioned in the text
- [ ] Figures numbered in the order they first appear
- [ ] Every figure and table has a full caption
- [ ] Tables sent as text, not screenshots
- [ ] Figures sent at full resolution, separately from the document
- [ ] Any figure I didn't create is flagged
- [ ] `.bib` file included; everything cited is in it, everything in it is cited
- [ ] DOIs included where they exist
- [ ] Abstract under 400 words and stands alone
- [ ] Title proofread; no full stop at the end
- [ ] Species names italicised, lowercase epithet
